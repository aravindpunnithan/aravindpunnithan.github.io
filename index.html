Aravind Punnithan

An iOS engineer with a longstanding interest in moral philosophy, technology ethics, and the slow craft of teaching.

01 About

I build software for a living and read philosophy because I cannot help it. By day I lead iOS engineering work on field-service and warehouse-management applications used by technicians and operators across several time zones. By the rest of the day I volunteer with IEEE, mentor students, and try to keep up a slow conversation with older books.

I grew up in Kerala and still live here, in a city that sits between a backwater and the Arabian Sea. Malayalam is my first language; English is the one I work in. I am wary of efficiency that has forgotten what it is for.

02 Practice

My day job is iOS — Swift, UIKit, the long tail of platform changes that shows up every September. I have spent several years on a single large field-service application, which is the kind of codebase that teaches you patience: payment hardware integrations, MDM constraints, offline sync, App Store review cycles, the regression you only notice in iOS 18.3.

I think of engineering leadership less as a role and more as a disposition: paying attention to the thing in front of you, writing things down so other people do not have to guess, and refusing to ship a fix you do not understand. The work I am most proud of is rarely the most visible.

03 Community

I serve as Vice Chair of the IEEE SSIT Kerala Chapter and as Organizing Secretary for ISTAS 2026, the IEEE International Symposium on Technology and Society, which we are bringing to Kovalam. SSIT — the Society on Social Implications of Technology — is the corner of IEEE I find most alive, because it insists on asking what for and at what cost before it asks how.

I also mentor and judge for student programs, occasionally run sessions on prompt engineering and AI literacy for undergraduates, and help organize hackathons when someone with energy and a venue appears. None of this is on a CV; all of it is the work.

04 Interests

I read in moral philosophy and the older wisdom traditions — Stoicism, Vedanta, the Christian contemplatives, whatever crosses the desk. I am drawn to writers who treat ethics as something one practises rather than something one solves: Iris Murdoch, Bernard Williams, Marcus Aurelius in the better translations, more recently Agnes Callard.

I am interested in the ethics of the tools I help build — what software does to attention, to the texture of work, to the people whose jobs it quietly reshapes. I do not have a finished position on any of this. The not-having is part of the point.

Outside that: walks, conversations that overrun, a slow attempt at keeping plants alive on a Trivandrum balcony.
